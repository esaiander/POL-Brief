Research Brief

Payer of Last Resort

Federal requirements for sequencing VR and Medicaid waiver funding in IDD employment services

NASDDDS / Prepared for IDD and VR agency leadership
Purpose of this brief

A shared factual foundation for the agencies that fund employment supports for people with intellectual and developmental disabilities.

This resource is for leaders in state Medicaid Home and Community-Based Services (HCBS) waiver programs and Vocational Rehabilitation (VR) agencies. It explains the federal payer of last resort requirement: what it is, what it requires, and what it does not require.

Each requirement is supported by direct citations to the relevant statutes and regulations. This brief also addresses misunderstandings that commonly arise in state coordination work. The goal is not to assign blame for those misunderstandings, but to provide a shared factual foundation from which leaders can build more effective policy and practice.

Both VR and waiver agencies face real operational constraints, including staffing limitations, high caseloads, Order of Selection policies, and service waitlists. The requirements described here exist within those realities. Understanding them clearly is the first step toward coordination systems that work for both agencies and for the people they serve.

A note on structure

The VR regulatory framework is more procedurally specific than the Medicaid payer of last resort provisions. As a result, this brief describes more VR-side procedures in detail. This reflects the structure of the regulations, not a judgment about which agency bears greater responsibility. Both agencies have obligations under this framework; the difference is in how specifically federal regulation prescribes the steps for meeting them.

The big picture

Three interlocking frameworks

This brief addresses three federal frameworks that work together to define when VR must engage, what that engagement requires, and when waiver responsibility begins. Understanding them is key to understanding why the payer of last resort obligation is less contested than it often appears in practice.

Framework 1
Payer of Last Resort
Medicaid-side obligation
What it says
Medicaid pays last. Before the waiver funds employment services, all other liable payers, including VR, must be identified and engaged.
Direction
Restricts the waiver from paying first.
42 U.S.C. § 1396a(a)(25)
42 C.F.R. §§ 433.139, 440.180(c)(3)(ii)
Framework 2
Comparable Benefits Rule
VR-side obligation
What it says
VR must look for other funders before spending its own funds, but key exemptions mean waiver services are not a comparable benefit for job-related services — job search, placement, retention, follow-up, and follow-along.
Direction
Does not excuse VR from engagement for employment services.
34 C.F.R. § 361.53
34 C.F.R. § 361.42(a)(3)
Framework 3
Extended Services Definition
Sequencing definition
What it says
Extended services are defined by when they begin, not what they look like. Waiver employment funding starts after VR formally closes. The handoff is sequence-based, not service-type-based.
Direction
Defines when waiver responsibility begins, not what it covers.
34 C.F.R. § 361.5(c)(19)
34 C.F.R. § 361.56(b)
How they connect

Framework 1 requires waiver agencies to confirm VR determination. Framework 2 confirms that waiver services are not comparable benefits for VR agencies.
Framework 3 defines the moment when waiver responsibility begins in a typical sequence for dually eligible individuals.
Together, they point in one direction: VR determination is required, it cannot be bypassed, and waiver funding begins only after VR closes when using a sequencing model.

TERMINOLOGY

Four terms to keep distinct

Four words recur throughout this brief and are easily conflated.  Some carry precise regulatory standards.

01Engagement

Any contact with VR, including a referral or intake conversation. Engagement alone does not satisfy the payer of last resort requirement.

02Application

The formal threshold defined in 34 C.F.R. § 361.41(b)(2): the individual has (1) requested services through any recognized method, (2) provided information necessary to initiate an assessment, and (3) is available to complete the assessment. 

03Determination

VR’s formal finding on eligibility, made after assessment. A determination can be: eligible (case opened, plan developed), ineligible (supported by clear and convincing evidence), or declined by the individual.

04Closure

Formal ending of a VR case. VR must issue a written closure letter stating the reason for closure and the person’s appeal rights. Closure creates the documentation the waiver agency requires. A case that was never opened cannot be formally closed.

1
Section One

What is payer of last resort?

Before a Medicaid waiver can authorize employment supports for a person with a disability, VR must have the opportunity to determine what it can offer. The statutory and regulatory provisions establishing this obligation are set out below.

FEDERAL RULE www.medicaid.gov/medicaid/eligibility-policy/coordination-of-benefits-third-party-liability

"By law, all other available third party resources must meet their legal obligation to pay claims before the Medicaid program pays for the care of an individual eligible for Medicaid. States are required to take all reasonable measures to ascertain the legal liability of third parties to pay for care and services that are available under the Medicaid state plan."

What this means

States may not design waiver programs or authorization processes that allow Medicaid to pay for employment services before VR has had the opportunity to determine its role. If a state has no mechanism to confirm VR engagement before authorizing waiver employment supports, that practice may not meet this requirement.

The statute at 42 U.S.C. § 1396a(a)(25) contains several operative subparagraphs that together create a comprehensive enforcement framework.

A

Reasonable measures. States must take all reasonable measures to ascertain the legal liability of third parties. This is an active obligation, not a passive one.

B

Reimbursement. States must seek reimbursement to the extent of the third party’s legal liability.

H

Acquired rights. When a Medicaid beneficiary receives services, the state is considered to have automatically acquired the individual’s rights to payment from any liable third party.

FEDERAL RULE 42 C.F.R. § 1396a(a)(25)(A)

The State or local agency administering such plan will take all reasonable measures to ascertain the legal liability of third parties (including health insurers, self-insured plans, group health plans (as defined in section 607(1) of the Employee Retirement Income Security Act of 1974 [29 U.S.C. 1167(1)]), service benefit plans, managed care organizations, pharmacy benefit managers, or other parties that are, by statute, contract, or agreement, legally responsible for payment of a claim for a health care item or service) to pay for care and services available under the plan-

What this means

This regulation puts the statute into practice. The phrase “all reasonable measures” requires active identification and pursuit of liable third parties, not passive receipt of claims. States that do not identify VR as a liable party before authorizing waiver employment services risk falling short of this standard.

FEDERAL RULE 42 C.F.R. § 433.139(b)(1)

If the agency has established the probable existence of third party liability at the time the claim is filed, the agency must reject the claim and return it to the provider for a determination of the amount of liability. The establishment of third party liability takes place when the agency receives confirmation from the provider or a third party resource indicating the extent of third party liability. When the amount of liability is determined, the agency must then pay the claim to the extent that payment allowed under the agency’s payment schedule exceeds the amount of the third party’s payment.

FEDERAL RULE 42 C.F.R. § 433.139(d)(2)

Except as provided in paragraph (e) of this section, if the agency learns of the existence of a liable third party after a claim is paid, or benefits become available from a third party after a claim is paid, the agency must seek recovery of reimbursement within 60 days after the end of the month it learns of the existence of the liable third party or benefits become available.

Together, these provisions mean the waiver agency must actively identify VR as a liable party. It cannot simply accept a service request at face value, and it has acquired the individual’s right to seek VR payment. Failure to act on these obligations potentially has direct financial consequences under federal regulation.

FEDERAL RULE 42 C.F.R. § 433.140(a) · FFP Penalty

Federal financial participation is not available for amounts attributable to a failure to fulfill third-party liability requirements under §§ 433.138 and 433.139.

What this means

Federal matching funds are at risk when a state Medicaid program pays for services that VR was liable to fund. States that authorize waiver employment services without confirming VR engagement may be claiming FFP on amounts they cannot lawfully claim. This is a programmatic integrity consideration for state agencies, independent of any individual service dispute.

COMMON MISUNDERSTANDING #1
“

Once a person is on the waiver, VR no longer has any role to play.

According to the rule

Waiver enrollment does not resolve the payer of last resort obligation permanently. Under 42 U.S.C. § 1396a(a)(25) and 42 C.F.R. § 433.139, the waiver agency must take reasonable measures to identify VR as a liable party whenever VR services might be available. This includes any point at which a person newly seeks employment supports or when employment circumstances change significantly. The obligation applies based on the availability of VR services, not on when the person enrolled in the waiver. An initial VR determination at enrollment does not cover later changes in employment status or service need.

Policy implication

The person-centered planning process, particularly at annual review, is the most natural point for confirming whether employment circumstances have changed in a way that requires VR to re-engage. Agencies may find it useful to include a standing prompt in planning documentation that asks whether the person has expressed new interest in employment or whether their situation has changed materially since the last VR determination.

2
Section Two · The Medicaid-Side Exclusion

Waiver habilitation may not duplicate VR

The payer of last resort principle is reinforced from the Medicaid side by a separate regulatory provision that directly governs what waiver habilitation services may include. This provision is independent of the comparable benefits rule on the VR side (discussed in Section 3). It is a condition of federal approval for the waiver itself.

FEDERAL RULE 42 C.F.R. § 440.180(c)(3)(ii) · Services Not Included

The following services may not be included as habilitation services under the waiver: vocational rehabilitation services that are otherwise available to the individual through a program funded under section 110 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 (29 U.S.C. 730).

What this means

This is the Medicaid-side mirror of the VR comparable benefits rule. If VR services are available to an individual under Section 110 of the Rehabilitation Act, those services may not be funded as waiver habilitation, regardless of whether the individual has actually engaged with VR. The waiver agency is responsible for confirming VR availability before authorizing. Authorizing waiver habilitation for employment without this determination does not meet the conditions for federal financial participation in the waiver.

GUIDANCE CMCS Informational Bulletin · September 16, 2011

Updates to §1915(c) Waiver Instructions Regarding Employment and Employment-Related Services

CMS issued this bulletin to clarify how states may cover employment and employment-related services in §1915(c) HCBS waivers. It establishes a three-part test that states must satisfy before using waiver funding for employment services.

1

Service definition. The waiver service definition must specifically explain that the service does not include services available under Section 110 of the Rehabilitation Act.

2

Pre-authorization assurance. The state must confirm that VR services are not available to the participant before authorizing waiver employment services.

3

Documentation. The record must document that VR services were not available under Section 110 before waiver services were authorized.

The bulletin also defines core waiver employment service categories — Supported Employment (Individual), Supported Employment (Small Group), Prevocational Services, and Career Planning — and explicitly prohibits waiver funding for employment in facility-based or sheltered work settings. This guidance is not codified in regulation but represents CMS’s formal interpretation of waiver approval standards and has been applied in waiver reviews and renewals since 2011. The Medicaid-side prohibition aligns with the VR-side outcome standard: 34 C.F.R. § 361.5(c)(15) defines the only employment outcome VR may fund as competitive integrated employment (as defined in § 361.5(c)(9)) or supported employment leading to CIE. Both systems point at the same outcome standard from different statutory bases.

COMMON MISUNDERSTANDING #2
“

VR engagement is VR’s responsibility. If VR did not engage the waiver agency has no separate duty to confirm VR engagement before authorizing employment supports.

According to the rule

Both agencies share responsibility. Medicaid regulations require the waiver agency to take reasonable measures to identify liable third parties before authorizing services. This is an affirmative obligation on the waiver agency, not merely a passive expectation that VR will show up. When waiver agencies authorize employment supports without VR documentation, the reasonable measures standard under 42 C.F.R. § 433.139 may not be met, regardless of what happened on VR’s end.

Policy implication

The affirmative obligation under 42 C.F.R. § 433.139 sits with the waiver agency, not VR. Because the Medicaid-side standard is “reasonable measures” rather than a prescribed procedure, the interagency agreement is the mechanism where both agencies define the operational steps together. That agreement is where waiver-side responsibilities such as initiating referrals, tracking referral status, and defining acceptable documentation types become concrete and enforceable.

Sections 1 and 2 establish the Medicaid-side obligations. The next question is what happens on the VR side: when VR receives a referral, can it point to the waiver and decline to engage? The answer depends on a provision called the comparable benefits rule.

3
Section Three · The Comparable Benefits Rule

What it requires — and what it does not

Before providing most services, VR is required under 34 C.F.R. § 361.53 to determine whether comparable services and benefits are available from another source. If they are, VR must use those comparable benefits before spending its own funds. This requirement exists to prevent duplication of federally funded services and to manage VR’s finite state allotment responsibly.

In the context of VR and Medicaid waiver coordination, the comparable benefits rule is frequently misunderstood in one specific and consequential way: it is sometimes used as a rationale for to frame VR engagement as a detour for a person who already receives waiver services. The reasoning goes that because the waiver provides employment supports, comparable benefits are available and VR need not engage.

This reasoning inverts the payer of last resort framework. The waiver is Medicaid, which is explicitly the payer of last resort. A comparable benefit is a resource VR may draw on instead of its own funds. Medicaid, as the payer of last resort, is by definition the last resource called upon, not an alternative first source. It cannot logically be the comparable benefit that excuses VR from determining their programatic liability through an eligibility determination. More directly, the regulation itself resolves this question through a set of explicit exemptions.

FEDERAL RULE 34 C.F.R. § 361.53 · Comparable Benefits

The vocational rehabilitation services portion of the Unified or Combined State Plan must assure that prior to providing any vocational rehabilitation services, except those services listed in paragraph (b) of this section, to an eligible individual or to members of the individual’s family, the State unit must determine whether comparable services and benefits exist under any other program and whether those services and benefits are available to the individual unless such a determination would interrupt or delay— (1) the progress of the individual toward achieving the employment outcome identified in the individualized plan for employment; (2) an immediate job placement; or (3) the provision of vocational rehabilitation services to any individual who is determined to be at extreme medical risk.

What this means

The comparable benefits requirement is a cost management provision in the VR regulations, not an eligibility or access restriction. It governs how VR allocates its finite state allotment when another program could fund the same service. It does not authorize VR to decline to open a case, conduct an assessment, or engage with a referral on the basis that other services exist.

Key terms for this section
Comparable benefit

A service or benefit available from another program that could meet the same need VR would otherwise fund. VR must check for comparable benefits before spending its own funds on most services.

Exempt service

A category of VR service that is excluded from the comparable benefits check entirely. VR may not delay or deny these services based on the existence of another funder.

Bridge provision

The requirement under § 361.53(c)(2) that VR must continue providing services when a comparable benefit exists on paper but is not yet available to the individual in practice.

Services explicitly exempt from the comparable benefits determination

Section 361.53(b) lists categories of service that are explicitly exempt from the comparable benefits requirement. For these services, VR may not delay or deny provision based on the existence of another funder. The exempt categories are defined in regulation and are not subject to state discretion.

Exempt service category

VR must accept and process a referral regardless of what assessments a waiver agency or provider has already conducted. A waiver-funded assessment is not a comparable benefit that substitutes for VR’s own eligibility determination.

This is the most consequential exemption for VR and waiver coordination. The regulation groups job search and placement assistance, job retention services, follow-up services, and follow-along services together as a single exempt category. VR may not decline to provide or fund any of these services — including supported employment job coaching during the stabilization period (which falls within job retention and follow-along) — based on the availability of comparable services through the waiver. The exemption directly rules out the argument that waiver-funded employment services substitute for VR’s obligation under this category.
Counseling and Guidance
§ 361.53(b)(2)
VR counseling, including information and support services that help a person exercise informed choice about their employment goals, is exempt from the comparable benefits determination. Waiver care coordinators and provider organizations may offer related guidance, but their involvement does not substitute for VR counseling as a formal VR service and does not trigger the comparable benefits check.
Referral and Other Services
§ 361.53(b)(3)
Referral services designed to help a person secure needed services from other agencies are exempt. This includes coordination activities that connect the person to resources outside VR.
Rehabilitation Technology
§ 361.53(b)(5)
Rehabilitation technology, including telecommunications, sensory, and other technological aids and devices, is exempt. VR may not delay or deny assistive technology or adaptive equipment on the basis that comparable devices might be available through the waiver or another program. Because waiver agencies and VR may both fund technology-related supports, the exemption means VR cannot point to waiver-funded devices as a reason to withhold its own technology services.
Post-Employment Services
§ 361.53(b)(6)
Post-employment services that help a person maintain, regain, or advance in employment are exempt to the extent they consist of services listed in (b)(1) through (5), including job coaching, counseling, assistive technology, and follow-along. VR cannot defer these to the waiver on comparable benefits grounds simply because the person is working or receiving waiver services.
Service Delay Threshold
§ 361.53(a)
This is not a (b) exemption but a threshold condition built into the (a) requirement itself. VR must conduct the comparable benefits determination unless doing so would interrupt or delay the person’s progress toward their employment outcome. In practice, VR cannot use the comparable benefits process to stall services when a person has a job opportunity in front of them or when delay would cause a job loss.
COMMON MISUNDERSTANDING #3
“

If a person already has a job, they don’t need VR and can go straight to waiver employment supports.

According to the rule

VR’s post-employment, job retention, and follow-along services are explicitly exempt from the comparable benefits rule under § 361.53(b)(4) and (b)(6). This means VR must still engage with a person who is already employed. It cannot decline solely because the person has a job. These are VR-funded services for which the waiver cannot substitute until VR has made a formal determination.

Policy implication

Meeting the comparable benefits exemption requirement as written means that employment status alone cannot be used to bypass VR engagement. Agencies reviewing their intake and authorization workflows against this standard may find it useful to document how this scenario is currently handled and whether existing practice is consistent with 34 C.F.R. § 361.53(b)(4).

What the comparable benefits rule does apply to

The comparable benefits rule does apply to certain VR services less commonly at issue in IDD employment coordination: vocational training costs, post-secondary education, occupational licenses, and tools or equipment for self-employment, where another program such as workforce development or financial aid may be the primary funder. Knowing where the rule applies reinforces where it does not.

Connection to the payer of last resort framework

The comparable benefits rule and the payer of last resort requirement operate in the same regulatory space but in opposite directions.

There is no regulatory path under which waiver-funded employment services substitute for VR engagement for this population.

With the comparable benefits framework established, the next question is why VR is the legally liable first payer in the first place, and what that obligation requires.

4
Section Four

Why VR must engage before the waiver authorizes

The previous sections established what the Medicaid side requires (Sections 1–2) and confirmed that the comparable benefits rule does not give VR a way to defer employment services to the waiver (Section 3). This section answers the question those obligations raise: what is VR’s legal basis for being the first payer, and what does engagement actually require?

Vocational Rehabilitation is authorized under Title I of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973, as amended by the Workforce Innovation and Opportunity Act (WIOA) of 2014. VR is funded through a federal-state matching arrangement and is specifically designed to help people with disabilities prepare for, obtain, maintain, and advance in competitive integrated employment (CIE). Because VR is authorized and funded to provide employment services, it is considered a legally liable third party for purposes of Medicaid’s payer of last resort requirement.

This means that before Medicaid waiver employment supports can be authorized for a person with a disability, VR must be engaged to determine whether and what it can offer.

This does not mean the person must accept VR services, complete a full rehabilitation program, or wait indefinitely. VR must have the opportunity to make a formal determination. Once that determination is made and documented — whether VR can help, cannot help, or the person declines to participate — the waiver may proceed.

Why this framework serves both agencies

The payer of last resort framework is sometimes experienced as a constraint. It is worth recognizing that it also protects both agencies’ institutional roles. For VR, engagement with people with IDD who receive waiver services is the mechanism through which VR fulfills its mandate to serve people with the most significant disabilities. When VR routinely defers employment services to the waiver, VR’s relevance to this population diminishes, caseload data no longer reflects need, and VR’s justification for its own funding erodes. For the waiver agency, the framework protects against paying for services that another program is legally liable to fund, reducing FFP risk and freeing waiver resources for supports that only the waiver can provide. Effective coordination strengthens both agencies.

What VR engagement requires

Five federal provisions establish the legal infrastructure for VR engagement with Medicaid-enrolled individuals.

FEDERAL RULE 29 U.S.C. § 721(a)(11) · Rehabilitation Act, Title I

Each state VR plan must include procedures to ensure that the provision of VR services is coordinated with other state agencies and programs that provide services to individuals with disabilities, including Medicaid.

What this means

This provision establishes coordination as a state plan assurance rather than a discretionary practice. The implementing regulation at 34 C.F.R. § 361.24(g) makes this more specific: the state plan must name the State Medicaid agency, the State IDD agency, and the State mental health agency as coordination partners and describe the approach. Together, the statute and regulation move VR coordination from a general expectation to a documented, auditable commitment.

FEDERAL RULE 34 C.F.R. § 361.24(f) · Cooperative Agreement — HCBS Waiver Participants

The vocational rehabilitation services portion of the Unified or Combined State Plan must include an assurance that the designated State unit has entered into a formal cooperative agreement with the State Medicaid agency and the State agency with primary responsibility for individuals with intellectual and developmental disabilities, with respect to the delivery of vocational rehabilitation services — including extended services — for individuals with the most significant disabilities who have been determined eligible for home and community-based services under a Medicaid waiver, State plan amendment, or other Medicaid authority.

What this means

This is the most specific federal coordination requirement applicable to VR and the State IDD agency. It does three things that bear directly on the payer of last resort framework:

1

It requires a formal cooperative agreement, not a general state plan description; the agreement is itself a federal expectation.

2

It identifies the population by name — individuals with the most significant disabilities determined eligible for HCBS waiver services — the population at the center of most VR/DD coordination work.

3

It explicitly names extended services within the agreement’s scope — the service category that defines the handoff between VR-funded supported employment and waiver-funded long-term supports.

The provision does not prescribe operational steps, which is by design: the formal cooperative agreement is the vehicle through which the two agencies define those steps together. The broader collaboration requirement in § 361.24(g) sits alongside (f) and extends to other State agencies and broader integrated employment opportunities.

FEDERAL RULE 34 C.F.R. § 361.42(a)(2)–(3) · Presumption of Benefit & Presumptive Eligibility — SSI & SSDI
§ 361.42(a)(2) · PRESUMPTION OF BENEFIT

Presumption of benefit. The designated State unit must presume that an applicant who meets the eligibility requirements in paragraphs (a)(1)(i) and (ii) of this section can benefit in terms of an employment outcome.

§ 361.42(a)(3) · PRESUMPTIVE ELIGIBILITY — SSI & SSDI

Any applicant who has been determined eligible for Social Security benefits under title II or title XVI of the Social Security Act is - (A) presumed eligible for vocational rehabilitation services under paragraphs (a)(1) and (2) of this section; and (B) considered an individual with a significant disability as defined in § 361.5(c)(29).

What this means

This provision has significant practical effect on VR engagement timelines for people with IDD. Because the large majority of individuals with IDD who receive HCBS waiver services also receive SSI or SSDI, VR have the ability to open cases for these individuals without the delay of a full eligibility review. Combined with the (b)(4) exemptions covering job-related services established in Section 3, the comparable benefits analysis is effectively removed from most employment-related services for this population. The result is that for most waiver participants seeking employment, VR engagement should be straightforward and prompt.

FEDERAL RULE 34 C.F.R. § 361.53(b)(4) · Comparable Benefits Exemption

The following vocational rehabilitation services are exempt from the comparable benefits determination: job-related services, including job search and placement assistance, job retention services, follow-up services, and follow-along services.

What this means

As established in Section 3, this exemption means VR cannot use the comparable benefits process to defer employment services to the waiver. The exemption rules out that basis for declining engagement, regardless of what waiver services may be available to the individual. This is the provision that closes the loop: the Medicaid side requires VR engagement (Sections 1–2), and the VR side confirms that engagement cannot be avoided through the comparable benefits rule.

FEDERAL RULE 34 C.F.R. § 361.42(e) · Trial Work Experiences

Prior to any determination that an individual with a disability is unable to benefit from vocational rehabilitation services in terms of an employment outcome, the designated State unit must conduct an exploration of the individual’s abilities, capabilities, and capacity to perform in realistic work situations. The State unit must develop a written plan to assess the individual’s abilities through the use of trial work experiences, which must be provided in competitive integrated employment settings to the maximum extent possible. Trial work experiences include supported employment, on-the-job training, and other experiences using realistic integrated work settings. The State unit must provide appropriate supports, including assistive technology and personal assistance services, to accommodate the individual’s rehabilitation needs during the trial work experiences.

What this means

When VR needs to further assess whether a person with a significant disability can benefit from services, that assessment should happen through real work in integrated settings, not through an office-based or facility-based evaluation process. If a person already has a job or has a job opportunity in front of them, VR’s assessment can and should happen at that work site.

Supported employment and on-the-job training are explicitly listed as trial work experiences. This means VR cannot defer employment support to the waiver on the basis that it needs more time to assess the person’s capacity.

VR must also provide appropriate supports during the trial work period, including assistive technology and personal assistance. VR’s assessment process is designed to happen alongside employment, not before it.

A note on Order of Selection

Some VR agencies operate under an Order of Selection, which means that even after eligibility is determined, some individuals may not receive services immediately if they fall into a lower priority category. This is a real operational constraint. However, Order of Selection does not eliminate the engagement requirement. VR must still accept the application, make an eligibility determination, and assign a priority category. That process produces documentation. When an individual is placed on a waiting list under Order of Selection, VR issues a written notice that includes the individual’s priority category and their right to appeal. This documentation can serve as the basis for waiver authorization, because it reflects a formal VR determination, even though services have not yet begun. The payer of last resort requirement is satisfied by the determination, not by the delivery of VR services.

COMMON MISUNDERSTANDING #4
“

VR involvement will slow down employment supports and hurt the person’s chances of keeping their job.

According to the rule

The regulatory framework does not impose the delays commonly attributed to it. Under 34 C.F.R. § 361.42(a)(3), individuals who receive SSI or SSDI are presumptively eligible for VR without a separate eligibility review, removing the most common source of delay. Under 34 C.F.R. § 361.53(c)(2), VR must continue providing services if waiver services are not yet available, which eliminates gaps caused by timing mismatches. Under 34 C.F.R. § 361.42(e), even when further assessment is needed, VR must conduct that assessment through trial work experiences in competitive integrated settings and must provide supports during the assessment period. Concurrent waiver services for non-employment supports are not prohibited during VR engagement. Where delays do occur, they typically reflect coordination gaps rather than regulatory requirements.

Policy implication

Timing concerns are most effectively addressed through joint protocols rather than by bypassing VR engagement. The coordination obligation in 29 U.S.C. § 721(a)(11) requires VR to describe its approach to Medicaid coordination in its state plan, which provides a natural basis for joint timelines defining trigger points and handoff responsibilities for common scenarios.

With the legal basis for VR engagement established, the next section describes what the full funding sequence looks like in practice and the specific regulatory provisions that govern each phase.

5
Section Five

The funding sequence

Federal law establishes specific requirements about when VR must engage and when Medicaid waiver funding may be used for employment services. These requirements define a sequencing framework that governs most situations involving people with IDD who receive waiver services and are seeking employment.

It is worth noting that blending and braiding of funding — coordinating multiple funding streams to support a single person’s employment across different service types — are recognized coordination strategies endorsed by the nine-agency federal joint communication on competitive integrated employment (2022). Those approaches operate within, not in place of, the sequencing obligations described here.

The payer of last resort requirement does not create a rigid bureaucratic sequence that must be completed before any support can begin. It creates a framework for determining who pays for what, and when. Understanding this framework helps agencies design efficient coordination systems rather than compliance burdens.

COMMON MISUNDERSTANDING #5
“

This person has complex needs, so the waiver should begin funding long-term employment supports now — even though VR has not closed the case.

According to the rule

Two separate issues are often combined here. First, when may the waiver fund employment supports? The answer is governed by sequencing: waiver employment funding is appropriate after VR has formally closed the case under 34 C.F.R. § 361.56(b), not before. The legal trigger is case closure following a successful employment outcome, not a judgment about the difficulty or duration of the work required to reach that outcome. Second, what does “extended services” mean under 34 C.F.R. § 361.5(c)(19)? Extended services are defined by their position in the sequence, not by their character. They are the supports the waiver funds after transition from VR-funded supported employment. A service does not become an “extended service” because it resembles long-term support or because the person has complex needs. Until VR closes, the funding responsibility has not transferred.

Policy implication

Disputes about when waiver responsibility begins are most productively resolved by reference to case closure status rather than to the nature or intensity of the person’s support needs. Interagency agreements that define what constitutes a successful employment outcome for purposes of VR closure, and that establish a joint process for reviewing cases where closure timing is in question, provide a more durable resolution than case-by-case negotiation.

The sequencing framework answers who pays when. The next question is how VR’s determinations are documented and what procedural protections apply when those determinations are disputed.

6
Section Six

VR closure and due process requirements

The payer of last resort framework is not only about funding. It is also a due process framework. When VR makes a formal determination about a person’s case, that determination must be documented in a way that protects the person’s right to appeal. This requirement is not optional and cannot be waived through informal practice.

What formal VR closure requires

When VR closes a case — whether due to successful employment, ineligibility, or the person declining services — it must issue a written closure document that includes:

1

The reason for closure, stated specifically

2

The date of closure

3

Information about the person’s right to appeal the closure decision

4

The timeframe for requesting mediation or a fair hearing — 30 days from notice of closure

5

Information on how to request a review by the Client Assistance Program (CAP)

FEDERAL RULE 34 C.F.R. §§ 361.43–361.44 · Closure Requirements

VR must provide written notice of case closure to the individual that includes the reason for closure, the individual’s right to request mediation or a fair hearing, and the procedures for requesting such review. VR must continue providing services during the pendency of any appeal unless the individual has been found ineligible.

What this means

A verbal statement that a case is closed, or a form without appeal rights information, does not meet this standard. If a person receives documentation that lacks these elements, agencies should work with VR to issue corrected documentation before using it as the basis for a waiver authorization.

FEDERAL RULE 34 C.F.R. § 361.41 · Application & Determination of Eligibility

VR must determine eligibility based on a completed application, and must complete that determination within 60 days unless an extension is agreed to. Under § 361.41(b)(2), an individual is considered to have submitted an application when they have: (A) completed and signed an agency application form, completed a common intake form at a one-stop center, or otherwise requested services from the designated State unit; (B) provided information necessary to initiate an assessment to determine eligibility and priority for services; and (C) are available to complete that assessment.

What this means

This is the regulatory basis for the principle that pre-application screening forms cannot serve as the basis for waiver employment support authorization. The three-part definition in § 361.41(b)(2) establishes a precise threshold for what counts as an application: the person must have requested services through a recognized method, provided information sufficient to initiate an assessment, and been available for that assessment. Any process completed before these conditions are met does not constitute an application, and any documentation generated before this threshold has no formal regulatory status as a VR determination. The complement to this is 34 C.F.R. § 361.42(a)(4)(ii), which provides that completion of the application process is itself sufficient evidence of the individual’s intent to achieve an employment outcome and that no additional demonstration is required. Read together, these provisions establish that the application is both necessary and sufficient: pre-application screening adds a hurdle the regulation does not require, and post-application motivation documentation is regulatorily unnecessary.

FEDERAL RULE 34 C.F.R. § 361.42(a)(4)(ii)

Completion of the application process is itself sufficient evidence of the individual’s intent to achieve an employment outcome – no additional demonstration is required.

Intent – settled by applying

No motivation test, readiness screen, work-behavior checklist, or further demonstration may be added

Capacity – answered through work

Where genuine capacity questions exist, the regulation already assigns the method: trial work in real, integrated settings, with supports. § 361.42(e)

What the text settles

“They’re not ready for VR yet.” Readiness is not a regulatory category. Intent is established by the application; capacity is assessed through trial work when there are doubts.

COMMON MISUNDERSTANDING #6
“

If a person declines VR services, that settles it — VR’s role is done and the waiver can proceed.

According to the rule

A declination does not resolve the payer of last resort obligation for either agency, and the two most common scenarios require different responses. If a person declines to apply for VR at all, no case was opened and no formal determination exists. The waiver agency has no VR determination on file, and proceeding on that basis would not meet the standard under 42 C.F.R. § 433.139. If a person applies, a case is opened, and the person then declines services after application, VR must formally close the case with a written closure letter that includes the reason for closure and appeal rights information, as required under 34 C.F.R. §§ 361.43–361.44. That letter is what the waiver agency requires. If the person’s employment situation or support needs change at a later date, VR should be re-engaged as described in Common Misunderstanding #1.

Policy implication

The regulatory framework distinguishes between these two scenarios by what documentation exists. Agencies working to align practice with 42 C.F.R. § 433.139 and 34 C.F.R. §§ 361.43–361.44 will generally find it useful to have written policies that specify what is required in each scenario, who is responsible for producing the documentation, and how it is retained in the authorization record.

Resolving these requirements in practice requires clear documentation standards. The following section defines what constitutes adequate VR documentation for waiver employment support authorization, and what does not.

Section Seven

Documentation requirements for waiver authorization

Payer of last resort compliance is ultimately demonstrated through documentation. The standard below defines what the waiver agency must have on file before authorizing employment supports. It is set by the regulatory requirements in 34 C.F.R. §§ 361.41–361.44 — not by any particular form.

✓Acceptable document types
VR closure letter
Post-application; includes reason and appeal rights.
VR ineligibility determination
Post-application; includes appeal rights.
Written declination of services
Post-application; person formally declined after the case was opened and VR issued a closure letter.
All must reflect a formal VR process opened under 34 C.F.R. § 361.41(b)(2).
×Not acceptable

Any documentation generated before a formal VR application was filed:

×Pre-application screening forms
×Intake screening notes
×Informal VR contact records
×Verbal or written staff statements not reflecting an open case

A note on pre-application screening forms and referrals

Pre-application screening forms

Some states use pre-application intake or screening tools that generate a written output before a formal VR application is filed. Where these exist, the output does not constitute a VR determination: no case has been opened, no eligibility decision has been made, and no appeal right exists. Documentation generated at this stage does not satisfy the payer of last resort requirement, regardless of its format or the language it uses. This is not a critique of screening tools as intake instruments — the issue arises specifically when their outputs are used as a substitute for formal VR case closure documentation.

Referrals

A referral to VR confirms that contact was initiated but does not establish that VR engaged, opened a case, or made a determination. A referral record in the waiver file, without documentation of a formal VR process, does not satisfy the payer of last resort requirement. It may indicate that the referral was not followed through and that VR engagement should be re-initiated before authorization proceeds.

COMMON MISUNDERSTANDING #7
“

A checklist or screening form from VR is sufficient documentation for waiver employment support authorization.

According to the rule

Documentation generated before a formal VR application is filed does not satisfy the payer of last resort requirement, regardless of its format. The applicable standard is set out in 34 C.F.R. § 361.41(b)(2) and §§ 361.43–361.44 and is described in the contrast above.

Policy implication

Defining acceptable documentation types in advance — rather than resolving the question at the point of each authorization — is the approach most consistent with the reasonable measures standard in 42 C.F.R. § 433.139. An interagency agreement is the appropriate vehicle for this.

8
Section Eight · In Summary

What this brief establishes

Four points carry the weight of everything above. The first three state the rules; the last names the instrument through which agencies put them into practice.

What federal law requires

Medicaid is the payer of last resort by statute. VR is the funder of first resort for employment services within its authorized scope. Waiver habilitation may not fund what VR is available to provide. Neither agency can lawfully redirect an individual to the other while refusing to provide services itself.

What documentation is required

A formal VR closure letter, issued post-application, containing the reason for closure and appeal rights information. Pre-application screening forms, informal contact notes, and verbal statements do not satisfy this standard. If documentation is missing required elements, the waiver agency should work with VR to obtain corrected documentation before proceeding.

Why the interagency agreement matters

The Medicaid-side standard is “reasonable measures”; the VR-side regulations specify detailed processes but do not dictate how the two agencies coordinate. The interagency agreement fills both gaps — defining who initiates referrals, what documentation is acceptable, how timing is managed, and how disputes are escalated. The cooperative agreement requirement at 34 C.F.R. § 361.24(f) makes it a federal expectation, not a discretionary practice.

Companion resource

This brief is paired with a field-level reference, Common Misconceptions in VR/DD Coordination, which addresses misconceptions outside the funding-sequencing frame — eligibility, integration of services and settings, and informed choice. Use the two together: this brief for agency leadership and policy alignment, the companion for field staff, joint training, and technical assistance.

The goal has not changed

The payer of last resort requirement exists because employment is a right, and sustaining employment requires stable, well-coordinated funding. The rules in this brief ask VR to engage, assess, and serve. They ask the waiver agency to confirm, document, and coordinate. Neither set of obligations is optional — together they create the conditions under which people with IDD get consistent access to the employment supports they need, for as long as they need them.

These rules are not obstacles to that goal. They are the framework that makes it possible.
